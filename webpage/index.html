Heatwave-induced capacity bottlenecks in European electricity grids

Extreme heat can increase electricity demand, change power-plant output and limit how much power transmission lines can carry. HEAT-Analysis examines how these effects interact and where they create bottlenecks in European grid models.

The study estimates near-term stress in grid models built from public data, rather than predicting outages in individual countries.

Enming Liang · Minghua Chen · Srinivasan Keshav

The problem

Why heat needs a whole-system view

Electricity supply depends on both available generation and the network's ability to deliver it. A heat-related constraint on a transmission line can therefore matter even when power is available elsewhere.

01

Cooling demand grows

More air conditioning increases the amount of electricity that must reach homes and businesses.

02

Available generation changes

Some thermal power plants lose available output as the air warms, while wind and solar production respond to the accompanying weather.

03

Line capacity responds to weather

Air temperature, sunshine and wind alter conductor heating and therefore the current that a route can carry within the modelled temperature limit.

The framework

Turning weather fields into a grid stress test

For each projected heatwave hour, the model tests whether the represented grid can meet demand under the corresponding weather conditions.

Method in brief

From extreme weather to network response

  1. 1

    Construct future heatwave weather

    The hourly patterns of selected ERA5 heat events from 2019, 2022 and 2024 are transferred to bias-corrected C3S/CORDEX climate baselines for 2026–2030 under RCP 4.5.

  2. 2

    Translate weather into grid inputs

    Each weather realisation determines electricity demand, renewable production, temperature-related generator availability and heating along individual line segments.

  3. 3

    Solve grid operation

    An alternating-current optimisation balances generation and power delivery within the represented operating and thermal limits. Demand that cannot be met is recorded as load shedding.

What the simulations show

Heatwave stress varies across represented grids

Applying the framework to eight country-level grid representations reveals different patterns of stress. Each is evaluated over 480 projected heatwave hours, accounting for differences in weather, network structure, generation, demand and storage.

Different grids, different bottlenecks

The represented French, Spanish and Italian systems show higher load-shedding risk. The German and UK models, together with several northern counterparts, remain less affected under the tested conditions.

Interconnection helps unevenly

Links to neighbouring systems provide relief only when those systems retain spare power and the connecting routes can deliver it to the constrained area.

Demand growth increases stress

In the Spanish analysis, higher base demand increases load shedding. The storage comparison varies only the initial charge of the represented fleet; future storage locations and capacity could produce different outcomes.

Spain: comparing methods

Why the modelling approach matters

The differences between grids raise a further question: how much does the choice of model affect the bottlenecks we identify? The Spanish case compares four approaches using the same 480 projected heatwave hours.

AC-OPF uses fixed transmission limits, while Quad-OPF approximates their temperature dependence. Iter-OPF is the study's two-update method; TD-OPF continues the temperature-dependent calculation until the results stabilise, providing a convergence reference.

In this comparison, AC-OPF and Quad-OPF yield less load shedding but allow some lines to exceed the modelled 90°C thermal limit. Iter-OPF captures the additional constraints and closely matches the TD-OPF reference, with a shorter running time. The important difference lies in local thermal bottlenecks, even though average line temperatures are similar.

What the results mean

Interpreting the results

Load shedding marks demand that the model cannot serve under the specified weather, infrastructure and operating constraints. Its location and extent help identify bottlenecks and explain how heat affects power delivery. These are conditional stress-test results, not predictions of national outages.

Network upgrades, new generation and storage, and operational measures could change the results. With more detailed grid and cost data, the framework could compare adaptation options such as demand response, transmission reinforcement, targeted storage and cross-border support.
